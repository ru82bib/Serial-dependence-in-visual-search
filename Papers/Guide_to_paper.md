# Maljkovic and Nakayama’s Study of Priming of Pop-Out (1994): An Overview of Experiments 1–9

Jari: this is a short overview of Maljkovic's work. In particular, Experiment 568 and the overall paradigm are both worth noting.

Maljkovic and Nakayama investigated how recent search experience affects the speed of finding a visually distinctive target. In the standard task, observers located an **odd-colored diamond** among distractors and reported whether its left or right side had been cut off. Color guided attention to the target, while shape determined the response. The main dependent measure was reaction time (RT) on correct trials. The authors called the benefit of repeating the attention-guiding feature **priming of pop-out (PoP)**.

## Experiment 1 — Fixed versus unpredictable target colors

- **Question.** How do target-color predictability and the number of distractors affect search performance?
- **Method.** In fixed-color blocks, the target remained the same color throughout. In mixed-color blocks, target and distractor colors could exchange roles unpredictably. Displays contained 2, 3, 5, or 11 distractors.
- **Hypothesis.** Based on earlier findings, the authors expected faster responses in fixed-color blocks. They also expected more distractors to facilitate search in mixed-color blocks, with little effect in fixed-color blocks.
- **Results.** Responses were faster in fixed-color blocks at every display size. Increasing the number of distractors shortened RT in mixed-color blocks, while RT remained approximately constant in fixed-color blocks. The difference between blocks was largest with two distractors, reaching approximately 100 ms for one observer and 200 ms for the other. Subsequent experiments therefore used two distractors.

## Experiment 2 — Predictability versus repetition

- **Question.** Does a fixed target color improve performance because observers can predict the next color or because they repeatedly select the same color?
- **Method.** The authors varied the probability that target and distractor colors would switch roles between trials. A switch probability of 0 produced constant repetition, 0.5 produced an unpredictable sequence, and 1 produced strict alternation.
- **Hypotheses.** An expectancy account predicted relatively fast responses at both extremes because repetition and strict alternation are equally predictable. A repetition-priming account predicted progressively slower responses as switches became more frequent.
- **Results.** RT increased with switch probability. All three observers were slowest during strict alternation, even though the next target color was fully predictable. This pattern supported a contribution from recent selection history beyond target-color predictability.

## Experiment 3 — The effect of the immediately preceding trial

- **Question.** Within a random sequence, how much does repeating the previous target color help? Does repeating the reported shape and response yield a comparable benefit?
- **Method.** The authors grouped trials according to whether the current target color repeated or switched relative to the previous trial. They separately grouped the same data according to whether the target’s cut side, and therefore the mouse response, repeated. Variants changed the visual hemifield of the display or reduced the difference between the two colors.
- **Hypothesis.** If priming primarily affects the feature that guides attention, target-color repetition should produce a larger and more consistent benefit than shape/response repetition.
- **Results.** Color repetition shortened RT by approximately 30–110 ms across observers and conditions. The advantage persisted across visual hemifields and with more similar colors. Shape/response repetition produced a much smaller, less consistent effect.

## Experiment 4 — Can deliberate anticipation reduce priming?

- **Question.** Can observers reduce the color-repetition advantage by actively anticipating the next target color?
- **Method.** Targets followed a predictable sequence of red, red, green, green. In the passive condition, observers simply responded to each display. In the active condition, they silently named the upcoming color before it appeared. The first target in each pair followed a color switch, while the second repeated the color.
- **Hypothesis.** If deliberate preparation can compensate for a color switch, actively anticipating the next color should narrow the RT difference between the first and second trials of each pair.
- **Results.** Active anticipation shortened RT overall. However, the second target in a pair retained its advantage, and this advantage did not diminish under active anticipation. Knowing and preparing for the next color therefore left a measurable effect of recent selection history.

## Experiment 5 — How long does a single trial influence search?

- **Question.** Can a target color encountered several trials ago still influence current performance?
- **Method.** For each lag \(k\), the authors compared current RT when the target colors on trials \(n\) and \(n-k\) matched versus differed. Random intervening trials were averaged over. Applying the same analysis to future trials provided a baseline for chance fluctuations.
- **Hypothesis.** A gradually decaying memory trace should produce the largest same-color benefit at lag 1 and progressively smaller benefits at longer lags.
- **Results.** Repeating the immediately preceding color shortened RT by about 45 ms. A same-color benefit remained visible at least five trials back, and the authors described the effect as fading over roughly five to eight trials. Trial lag and elapsed time varied together, so the experiment could not distinguish time-based decay from interference by intervening searches.

## Experiment 6 — Priming of spatial frequency

- **Question.** Does the same history effect occur when spatial frequency guides attention?
- **Method.** Observers searched for a Gabor patch whose spatial frequency differed from that of two distractors, such as a coarse grating among fine gratings. They then judged whether the target’s upper half was offset to the left or right of its lower half. The authors repeated the lag analysis from Experiment 5.
- **Hypothesis.** If priming applies to the feature used to select a target, repeating the target’s spatial frequency should facilitate performance across trials.
- **Results.** Observers responded faster when the current target had the same spatial frequency as a recent target. The benefit weakened with trial lag, broadly reproducing the color-priming pattern. By comparison, repetition of the reported offset and its associated response showed little systematic priming.

## Experiment 7 — Do successive repetitions produce cumulative benefits?

- **Question.** Does each additional same-color search contribute to performance beyond the benefit of the first repetition?
- **Method.** Observers completed runs of same-color targets of varying lengths. The authors plotted RT against the target’s ordinal position within a run and compared it with each observer’s RT in a fixed-color block.
- **Hypothesis.** If only the immediately preceding trial matters, performance should level off after the second target. If several recent trials contribute, RT should continue to decrease across the run.
- **Results.** Responses continued to accelerate beyond the second target. By approximately the eighth consecutive same-color trial, both observers had reached the speed observed in fixed-color blocks. This cumulative benefit helped explain the large block difference in Experiment 1.

## Experiment 8 — Target facilitation and distractor inhibition

- **Question.** How much of the repetition benefit comes from repeatedly selecting a target color, and how much comes from repeatedly ignoring a distractor color?
- **Method.** Using four colors, the authors created sequences in which either the target color stayed constant while distractor colors changed, or the distractor color stayed constant while target colors changed. They measured how RT developed over runs of 2, 4, or 6 trials.
- **Hypotheses.** Target facilitation predicted faster responses across repetitions of the target color. Distractor inhibition predicted faster responses across repetitions of the distractor color, even when the target color changed on every trial.
- **Results.** RT decreased in both conditions. Both observers showed a larger cumulative benefit from repeating the target color. The authors interpreted the two benefits as evidence that target facilitation and distractor inhibition both contribute to PoP, with a larger contribution from target facilitation in this task.

## Experiment 9 — Does priming transfer between the eyes?

- **Question.** Does a target color selected through one eye facilitate search when the next display is presented to the other eye?
- **Method.** A mirror stereoscope presented each three-item search display to one randomly selected eye. The authors compared the color-repetition benefit across consecutive trials presented to the same eye versus different eyes.
- **Hypotheses.** An eye-specific priming mechanism predicted a smaller benefit after an eye switch. A mechanism drawing on information shared across the eyes predicted comparable benefits in both conditions.
- **Results.** The same-color advantage was comparable for same-eye and different-eye trials in both observers. The authors described this as **complete binocular transfer**, consistent with a contribution from processing at or after the convergence of inputs from the two eyes. The behavioral result alone does not identify a specific cortical area.

## Overall conclusion

Recent search history influenced how quickly observers responded to a pop-out target. The benefit was strongest for the most recent trial, persisted across intervening searches, and accumulated with successive repetitions. Both the feature previously selected and the feature previously ignored contributed to performance. For research on serial dependence, the study establishes a **history-dependent change in search-task RT**. Whether recent selections also bias the *perceived value* of a current feature remains a separate empirical question.
